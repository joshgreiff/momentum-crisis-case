## MOMENTUM CASE – PART A

***Momentum in May 17, 2020 – Launch Day***

There was no rain in the forecast and the weather could not be more perfect. The sun was shining and traffic patterns were smooth. There were no wrecks along the delivery path, and no construction would slow down the trucks. Charlie Howth was a seasoned driver who had just completed six months of training, not to mention twenty previous years of service leading up to this day. A team of software engineers were looking on, patting each other on the back because they were so proud of all of their efforts leading up to this point.

It was the official launch day of the first professionally certified, semi-autonomous fleet of delivery trucks. Charlie, fifty-seven years old and the epitome of humble charm, waved to the cameras before climbing into the lead delivery vehicle.

A convoy of L4[i]\(*see exhibit #1 for explanation and exhibit #2 for images*) trucks extended behind him for a quarter of a mile, at the launch site in Etna, Ohio. The excitement was palpable. The first official delivery would be for Amazon, a contract that could endow Momentum—a global delivery company—with the transit crown (a local article recently pointed out). Momentum was now in the same league as Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Daimler, Tesla, Uber, Ford and Toyota; but what set the company apart was its dual commitment to both its people and its semi-autonomous vehicles (SAVs), emphasizing the Chief Executive Officer’s assurance of safety and best practices all the way down the value chain.

When asked for a statement for the local news about whether the company was planning to displace delivery drivers, CEO Sam (Samantha) Bennet stood tall and said, “We are not replacing humans with technology. We are marrying the strengths of our people with imminent technological advancements that will better our lives.”

Though the CEO could have said more about the opportunities for local fleet owners, or about the potential of people who are homebound being less reliant on inefficient delivery systems to get them medications and other time-sensitive products, Sam chose to keep her message simple. After all, this moment was historical for the company and the country. Momentum was leading the charge in a new era.

***Momentum Executive Leadership***

Sam Bennet was not new to the automotive industry when she stepped in as CEO of Momentum in early 2018. A global consultant who quickly became a VP and eventually the CEO, Sam had specialized in automotive and transportation trends for over a decade. She was considered an expert in the field and was consistently called upon by companies for help.

Poached from Boston Consulting Group, she had initially been hesitant to take on a new role that would force her to engage in the markets she knew so well; to Sam, who loved the work, it sometimes felt as though being on the sidelines was too secure. These markets could be volatile, and she enjoyed helping automotive companies make better decisions, but she always felt she could do more.

It was August 2017 when an executive recruiter reached out through LinkedIn and asked for a meeting with her to discuss an opportunity. Sam deleted the message. That same day the recruiter called her office and provided her assistant with a message to call her back. Sam told her assistant not to bother transferring any calls to her from the recruiter. Finally, a few days later, the recruiter called her at home. Sam answered with mild annoyance. The woman’s voice disarmed her immediately, however. She asked her about a project she had worked on with Momentum a few years ago, and said that the board had brought up her name as they were brainstorming potential replacements for the CEO role.

“Look, I’ll be honest with you, Sam. I have a lot of clients, and I don’t usually feel the urge to chase a person down, but you’re the one for this job. If you don’t think so after another conversation, I’ll leave you alone.” Impressed, Sam agreed. She had been a forward-thinking researcher, consultant and analyst who had worked her way up through deep knowledge and relentless problem-solving. Sam Bennet knew how to execute complex business strategies like no other candidate, and when she read about the company’s mission and vision, she began to see herself in the role.

Now, a little over two years later, Sam stood by very proud of the progress that Momentum had made to insert themselves as *the* major player in the industry by picking off global players such as FedEx and UPS. In 2019, *Fast Company* selected Momentum as the Most Innovative Company in the World, a prestigious honor that had previously been bestowed upon the likes of Google, Apple, Facebook, Warby Parker, and Meituan Dianping.  There were hundreds of Momentum employees standing by at launch day and hundreds more onlookers from the local community.

After joining Momentum in early January 2018, Sam assembled an exceptional leadership team that was forward thinking, took pride in the company’s “employee first” attitude, and willingness to do whatever it took to help the company succeed (*see exhibit #3 for the executive leadership team descriptions and exhibit #4 for the Board of Directors profiles*).

Sam replaced Marjorie (Margie) Simpson who stepped down but still remained chairman of the board. It was Margie who had taken the company to new heights during her 15 year reign as CEO and 32 years at the company. She was a hands-on leader who worked tirelessly. She was known for showing up to work at 4:30 AM and being the one who turned the office lights off at the end of the day. There were many days when she slept on the couch in her office because she was so focused on the success of the company. Margie was loved by the employees and the local community because of her presence and ability to relate to anyone - - - from the driver and mechanic all the way up to the C-Suite.  Her leadership allowed for a successful IPO in 2016 and several overnight millionaires.  She ultimately decided to step down from the CEO position so that she could ease into retirement and focus on more philanthropic endeavors.

***Back to Launch Day – May 17, 2020***

“Feels pretty good,” Sam said. Her father had been a truck driver, one who wouldn’t have liked the technology but would have cheered his daughter along nonetheless. A blue collar worker, Sam’s father had sacrificed everything to pay for his daughter’s college education at The Ohio State University.

“Three, two, one …. Take off!”

Charlie honked the horn, and a few drivers called out his name. The highway would be blocked off for the duration of the thirteen-mile trip from this warehouse to a distribution center in Columbus, Ohio. It would be hub-to-hub, with Charlie at the helm of an L2 delivery truck and commanding two additional L4 autonomous (driverless) trucks. The software onboard Charlie’s truck demanded a certain amount of expertise that Charlie and the other drivers were committed to learning, with some trepidation.

“It’ll be like playing the most important video game of my life,” one of the AVD (autonomous vehicle driver) trainees said. The training had been rolled out to all senior drivers, along with some of the newcomers who volunteered. In five years, Sam hoped to train all her current drivers – not limiting herself by the shortsightedness of the “innovation versus people” attitude many other companies adopted.

“While we are forward thinkers, and this drives everything we do, we will always put our people first,” she often said. Her team believed every word.

***Momentum Company History***

Founded by Willard Wilson in 1922 as a small common carrier delivery company in Etna, Ohio, Momentum was born in the shadow of Merchants Parcel Delivery Services, which would later become UPS. Momentum was never on the same growth trajectory. The company did not gain steam outside of the Midwestern United States until World War II when it began to offer women employment in both part and full-time roles.

After the war, the company made it a point to offer jobs to soldiers who were navigating a new reality in the United States, which coined it “The Family Company,” because everyone in the family worked there. There was a people-centric view from the inside out, and customers appreciated it. The early focus on diversity and inclusion set up for a strong foundation for the organization.

Momentum had become a staple in the Etna community, a small town that had grown to 18,000 people. The organization always made it a priority to invest in the schools and several local nonprofits. The community engagement arm of the company consistently found ways to give back. Everyone in the area either had a family member or a friend who worked for the company in a “high paying job.” The mayor once said, “I don’t know where our town would be without Momentum supporting us every step of the way.”

With retail always at the core of its business, Momentum began to grow slowly and steadily thanks to dedicated employees and a supportive atmosphere. The company was passed down two generations before landing in the capable hands of Marjorie Simpson, who would begin to expand operations of the private carrier company to a coast-to-coast service that people, notably suburban families, came to rely on.

Simpson upheld the company’s mission and values (see exhibit #5), but she also saw endless potential, and began to negotiate contracts outside of the United States. Under Sam Bennet’s predecessor, the company went public in 2016. With a large influx of capital as a result of the initial public offering, Momentum expanded to invest in the larger trucking industry and began to focus even more on technology, pharmaceuticals, and trade. Its biggest clients remained retailers, who were eager to be on the cutting edge of tomorrow’s marketplace and didn’t necessarily have the necessary warehouse space to hold large amounts of inventory on site. Momentum was growing fast, but Simpson tended to be hesitant about all of the new technologies that seemed to be popping up. Her philosophy was for the company to be a “fast second mover” by quickly adopting other organizations’ proven technologies instead of blazing the path for them.

As Simpson felt the organization was slipping behind due to the high speed changes with technology, she decided to retire at the first of the year in 2018 handing over the reins to Sam Bennet, whom she advocated for. Simpson remained Chairman of the Board after she retired, and served as a supportive mentor. She still had a significant stake in Momentum, and made it a habit to call Sam at least once a week outside of the Board of Director’s meetings. Her advice and guidance was welcomed with open arms; meanwhile, she had some reservations about the technology shifts that were happening industry-wide so quickly. Autonomous vehicles felt like science fiction to her, and she was dubious when it came to Globex.

Unable to watch the launch, Marjorie Simpson was texting Sam. “I don’t know if I should be excited or terrified about the future,” she wrote. “Either way, I’m glad you are keeping up with the times. Well done.”

***Globex: The acquisition and Momentum’s entry into autonomous delivery***

Standing next to Sam at the launch, Brian, endearingly referred to as “The Brain” by his contemporaries, looked terrified. While Sam was the charismatic CEO, Brian Chambers, Founder & CEO of Globex, was the pragmatist. A scientific wunderkind who had twelve patents in the mobile technology and drone space by the time he was twenty-one, Brian saw the future in zeroes and ones. He was brilliant but insensitive when it came to managerial tasks—or any interaction with other people, for that matter.

Momentum would be the first company to launch a small fleet of semi-autonomous delivery (SAD) trucks, and today was the test run. Brian had been waiting for this day. CNN and Fox alike called this day an exercise in forward-thinking, two companies that joined together as one who would be setting the stage for a next-generation delivery industry. Momentum had broken ground when it pledged to online retail giant, Amazon, that it would be able to make deliveries to their end users in less than eight hours. This promise was bolstered by the company’s acquisition of Globex, shortly after Sam became CEO of Momentum (*see exhibit #6 for Bloomberg’s article about the acquisition*). Brian’s small tech start-up, Globex, was founded in 1999 and had steadily grown through the tech transformations of the 2000’s. What began with his friend and co-founder in his grandmother’s garage, had grown to almost 2,200 employees. Globex was based in San Francisco, California, but after the acquisition, became co-located between the Bay Area and Etna, Ohio – two locations that couldn’t be more different. Globex chose Momentum as a partner because of its longevity, commitment to forward-thinking, and exciting new investments in autonomous options. The two companies were in agreement that passenger vehicles were not where the technology would make the biggest impact—it was all about delivery vehicles. Globex kept their name as a subsidiary of the parent company, Momentum. Globex was the sole function that was responsible for the IT and technological strategy of the entire organization and the relationship was excellent.

Towards the end of 2019, the U.S. House of Representatives and U.S. Senate passed a bill that lifted final restrictions on introducing semiautonomous commercial trucks to the nation’s highways. Momentum invested almost $700 million toward

Globex who was also working on a line of drones to deliver items of high-importance, such as prescriptions, in less than two hours. It was ground-breaking and the backlash from those drivers who could potentially soon be laid off was still minimal. They had been under the assumption that each would have a fleet due to increasing customer spending. At least it had seemed that way, till now.

Sam had strategically given Brian a large office facing a small garden in the back of the corporate headquarters, where no passersby would accidentally stumble into his office. To walk into Brian’s office was like walking into a crime scene. Stained and over-worn books were stacked on his desk, alongside a few days’ worth of clothing. He often slept in his chair, nodding off at two in the morning after becoming engrossed by some new process or possibility. He kept a notebook, in fact, called process and possibility, and he refused to keep any of his important notes online. When Sam gave him an external hard-drive to keep in his safe, he turned it away. The slight paranoia around technology from one of the brightest tech scientists in the world was a bit disconcerting, but Sam chose to brush it off as eccentricity. During a typical month, Brian would spend three weeks at the Etna headquarters and one week at the San Francisco office.

***Back to Launch Day – May 17, 2020***

Are you ok? Asked Brian of Sam. “That man has been through training, so yes. I am 100% confident he will be able to manage these vehicles. The technology is essentially idiot-proof. What concerns me more is what happens next. This is being broadcast. I did not agree to that. I should have been informed.”

“We just found out ourselves. I would’ve worn a different suit had I known.”

Brian would often pretend to smile when Sam joked with him, but not today. “That is a lot of data. Nearly a gigabyte a second—the route, the location, the delivery itself, which means customer data as well as end user data to a certain extent. We need to be safe. We need to be conscientious before sharing what we’re doing with the world. We don’t know who is watching.”

The conclusion of launch day was a major success. Amazon could not have been more pleased and the publicity for Momentum was nationwide. Sales associates at Momentum became overwhelmed with customer inquiries from people interested in having Momentum handle the logistics portion of their business. The stock went up almost ten points that day.

***Initial Challenges: Technology Shifts Business***

It was a week later after launch day that Charlie Howth put in his two weeks’ notice to do his very first Today Show interview and then get started on a book he’d been commissioned to write. The public’s interest in autonomous vehicles was at an all-time high now that it was finally becoming a reality. Sam couldn’t help but open his prior employee’s Instagram feed.

“I can’t believe he left us after two decades,” Sam told the HR Manager, Joshua Scroll.

“Charlie was the first fleet manger, a sharp guy, but we need to think about training everyone now, or rethinking our roles within the company. We simply don’t need as many delivery drivers, and if they’re not trained soon, they’ll be dead weight,” Joshua said.

“How are the trainings going so far?” asked Sam.

“Expensive. And … not well. I’ll be honest with you, Sam. Most of these drivers are not as excited as Charlie. Some don’t have a tech bone in their bodies. They aren’t even willing. What we need is people with tech expertise. This is the future, after all, not the past.” The subtext of the situation did not escape Sam. These men and women were “old school” drivers who weren’t excited about the change. They liked what they did and many of them had been doing it for years. Much of the change had been met with resistance.

Although Sam didn’t like what she was hearing, she knew Joshua was right. A company can only invest in people who invest in themselves. Then again, what would her father have done?  Joshua was graduating with his Master’s degree in Human Resource Management that spring, and he would eventually be a strong candidate for the CHRO role in the company given his drive and commitment. He had been there for almost ten years, and his innovative spirit and relentless honesty had endeared Sam.

This was not the first conversation about the shifts that would be necessary with such staggering changes to the delivery system. Sam agreed that all drivers should be trained, but she also knew Brian’s take on these things and realized that data breaches would be a serious concern. She tried to imagine her father going through such an intense technology-based training, which now included almost nine months for fleet managers and seven months for drone delivery. As competitors were quick to see the launch and kick their own initiatives into hyper-speed, Sam had called an all-hands meeting in which she and Brian announced to the company their strategic plan. Within the next five years, Momentum would:

1. Become the primary delivery partner for retail, electronics, and pharmaceutical giants Amazon, Walmart, and Bayer North America
2. Expand technology acumen by training 75% of the current workforce
3. Support Globex with the necessary capital needed to remain cutting edge
4. Keep the commitment to current employees by offering robust training to enable the company’s seasoned employees to gain opportunity in the changing marketplace

Given Momentum’s current financial position (see exhibit #7), these goals appeared to be well within reach for the organization to achieve.

***Industry Challenge: Keeping up with Demand***

By the end of the first quarter of 2021, Momentum’s profits and growth were soaring, but the company had to make a few tough decisions. Momentum had achieved its strategic goals, becoming the primary delivery partner to most major retailers and the acquisition of Globex proved to be paying off as tech was serving as a key differentiator against the competition. The only wavering point was personnel.

To keep revenue and profits at an all-time high, and profit margins soaring, Sam knew that Momentum had to adopt an increasing number of SAVs and up her investment in L5 technologies, which many argued meant hiring people with tech degrees and downsizing or phasing out personnel. Though they were on top today, other companies were close behind.

Bolstered by rising environmental consciousness in the U.S., not to mention the 10-15% decrease in transit costs year-over-year due to lack of downtime made by driver stops, made for a strong adoption rate, despite the high cost of buy-in. Every delivery service who would be in business by 2030 was investing in automation on some level, even those who had originally claimed it would be destructive to society.

***Challenge: Avoiding Layoffs, Rising Costs and Stringent Regulations***

The day would never come, Sam told herself. Yet here it was, a solid case for major layoffs. The sad fact was, as Brian had brought up in most board meetings, training costs were too high and laborious to keep up with demand. Further, as technologies continued to evolve, it became more and more important that they hire a team of robotics and automation engineers to replace many of the drivers.

“The salaries will basically be a wash since we can hire one technology manager for every three or four drivers,” Brian had argued. Sam found herself in a dilemma, but the numbers (see Exhibit #8) were clear. She spent endless nights poring over the data. There were competitors biting at Momentum’s heels who didn’t care about displacing drivers. Sam could offer these men and women decent packages if they’d been with the company for more than ten years.

***Odd Occurrences***

In mid-March 2021 deliveries across the U.S. were picking up, and Sam had paused her decision to lay off drivers until after mid-year.

She got word from COO, Maria Mason, that she had some concerns about the technology. After a meeting with Globex leadership and the executive team, it was revealed that drivers had been reportedly laughing off some odd occurrences with the technology in some of the L4 vehicles. One report out of Indiana had arrived in which a truck’s horn was honking nonstop. A warehouse operator said that mid-delivery, a truck in San Jose began to blast pop music. Other trucks had blinkers going off at random times and still others would suddenly break slightly before accelerating.

Brian and Momentum’s CIO, D.L. Johnson, assured the team that they would get to the bottom of the glitches. They inspected the trucks in question and fast-tracked a series of randomized quality control checks. It didn’t seem to be a big deal, but no more than two weeks after the last incident, when quality control reports were coming back saying everything was fine, the ground beneath Momentum began to crack.

### *Exhibit #1: Levels of autonomous driving*

- **Level 0:** The most   basic of levels. The driver (human) controls everything: steering, brakes,   throttle, and power.
- **Level 1:** This   driver-assistance level means that most functions are still controlled by the   driver, but a specific function (like steering or accelerating) can be done   automatically by the car.
- **Level 2:** At least   one driver assistance system of "both steering and acceleration /   deceleration using information about the driving environment" is   automated, like cruise control and lane-centering.
- **Level 3:** Drivers are   still necessary, but are able to completely shift "safety-critical   functions" to the vehicle, under certain traffic or environmental   conditions.
- **Level 4:**    "Fully autonomous." Designed   to perform all safety-critical driving functions and monitor roadway   conditions for an entire trip." It is important to note that this is   limited to the "operational design domain (ODD)" of the   vehicle—meaning it does not cover every driving scenario.
- **Level 5:** This refers   to a fully-autonomous system that expects the vehicle's performance to equal   that of a human driver.

### *Exhibit #3: Momentum Executive Team Roles*

**CEO (Chief Executive Officer) – Sam Bennet**

The CEO is the highest-ranking executive in the company. The CEO is responsible for managing the organization at a high level. The CEO is the main point of communication to the board of directors.

*Tasks include:*

● Maintain communication with Board of Directors.

● Delegate tasks and streamline communications across the executive team.

**President, CEO & Co-Founder, Globex – Brian Chambers**

After Momentum’s acquisition of Globex, Brian was named as President & CEO of this part of the organization, and also serves as the Chief Information Officer. Brian reports directly into Sam and his function provides technological expertise, equipment, and personnel to equip Momentum’s fleet with cutting-edge automation software. The Globex President & CEO has a seat on the board.

*Tasks include:*

● Maintain communication with Board of Directors as well as Sam Bennet and the Momentum team.

● Delegate tasks and streamline communications surrounding technology.

● Oversee technical training programs.

**COO (Chief Operating Officer) – Maria Mason**

The COO is responsible for the daily operation of the company as well as designing the implementing business strategies that can increase operating efficiencies. The COO is second in command to the CEO.

*Tasks include:*

● Responsible for keeping the operations of the company running.

● Evaluate performance and opportunities to improve efficiencies.

**CFO (Chief Financial Officer) – Susan Bucks**

The CFO oversees financial and treasury based corporate functions. The CFO manages risk-based controls around financial decisions.

*Tasks include:*

● Review financial considerations of company.

● Implement processes to mitigate against risks.

**CMO (Chief Marketing Officer) – Aishwarya Rowe**

The CMO is responsible to marketing the business of the organization. This includes unifying the organization under one voice to stakeholders. It is crucial to remain consistent across communication channels (TV, News, Social Media, etc.).

*Tasks include:*

● Manage brand and consumer/stakeholder perceptions.

● Create marketing plan to advance brand, and align the plan with company goals.

**CHRO (Chief Human Resources Officer) – Rubin Oscar**

The CHRO is responsible for overseeing human capital decisions across the enterprise. This includes hiring, firing, and benefits.

*Tasks include:*

● Evaluate staffing decisions (hiring and firing).

● Keep a pulse on the overall organizational health.

### Exhibit #5: *Momentum’s Mission, Vision, and Values*

**Momentum Mission Statement**

As a company of thought leaders, Momentum prides itself on a customer-first delivery service that is forward-thinking, safe, and reliable.

**Momentum Vision Statement**

Momentum is driving its customers forward toward a world full of possibility. We put people first by thinking a step ahead. Innovative, sustainable, and secure, Momentive aspires to lead the world in deliveries and supply chain management.

**Momentum Values**

- **People** – Recognizing the needs of modern-day consumers, we think about our products and services as pivotal in the human ecosystem. We are dedicated to getting people and products where they need to go by identifying needs and being relentless problem solvers. We are obsessed with our people.
